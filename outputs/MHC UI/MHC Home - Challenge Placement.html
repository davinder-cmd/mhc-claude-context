Challenge placement — team vs. peer

The two challenge types have completely different rhythms. Team is a major company event with a fixed run; peer is a user-to-user dare measured in days. Each gets its own placement strategy across its lifecycle so the home page never feels stuck on a banner.

Why a 5-stage lifecycle, not "always in hero"

The challenge runs ~5 weeks end-to-end. Owning the hero for that whole stretch trains users to ignore it. Treating it like a campaign with deliberate moments of attention keeps each surface earned.

The pattern

  • Hero takes over only at the two beats that need a decision: announcement ("join now") and final stretch ("finish it"). 2 days + 3 days = 5 hero days out of ~35.
  • Slim banner handles the steady-state nudges: "you can still join" during the join window, "see your results" after it ends. Banner is dismissable per user (D1 ×) so it doesn't become wallpaper.
  • In-progress rail handles the activity period — same pattern as DCP/Journey, with the days-left rail variant. The challenge becomes one item among the user's ongoing work.

Server contract

  • challenge.stage ∈ announce | join_window | active | final_3 | wrap_up
  • Hero placement is decided server-side: announce & final_3 push to hero; everything else lets the focus program keep the hero.
  • User can dismiss the slim banner; banner respects dismiss for the remainder of that stage.

Why peer challenges live in two places

Peer challenges have a before and an after:

  • Before accepting: the invite is the action. It needs to read like a notification — sender's name, what they're asking, accept / decline. That's the Pending invites block at the top of home (above the hero, below the greeting).
  • After accepting: it's just another time-boxed thing the user is in the middle of. Slot it into the in-progress rail with the days-left variant and a "vs. {opponent}" tagline. It competes with DCPs, Journeys, and team challenges for one of the three rail slots.

Why not the hero — even at invite

Hero is for your commitments. A peer invite is someone else's commitment to you. Putting it in the hero is jarring and pre-empts the focus the user actually opted into. The Invites block is high in the page hierarchy without taking the marquee.

Inclusion priority within the rail

When the rail fills up, peer challenges with daysLeft ≤ 1 outrank focus-matched DCP. Otherwise, default ordering wins. The user accepted this — they want to know how it's going.

Auto-handling end states

  • Won / lost / tied: when the challenge ends, replace the rail card with a one-off result card for 24h ("You won! +50 pts"), then drop it.
  • Expired invite: 7-day TTL on pending invites; system silently removes from the Invites block after.
  • Opponent dropped out: show in rail with "{Opponent} stepped out" tagline + a "challenge someone else" link.

Server contract

  • peerChallenge.state ∈ invite_pending | active | finished | expired
  • invite_pending renders into the Invites block; active renders into the in-progress rail; finished renders for 24h then drops; expired never renders.

Summary

Team challenges get the hero only at announcement and final 3 days. Everything else (join window, activity period, wrap-up) lives in slim banners or the in-progress rail. Hero never owns more than 5 days of a 35-day campaign.

Peer challenges never own the hero. Pending invites get a dedicated Invites block at the top of home. Active peer challenges drop into the in-progress rail with a "vs. {name}" tagline and the days-left variant.

One template, three placements: hero · banner · rail card. Server flips the placement based on stage (team) or state (peer). The home page never feels stuck because the placement moves as the challenge ages.
