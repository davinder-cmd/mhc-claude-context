LifeForce · DEM-35 · UX audit

A UX audit of the current LifeForce experience

A factual look at how the enrollment experience works today — what's strong, and where it can improve — so the experience can match the program behind it.

Prepared as a reference for the team. It reviews the live production screens against established usability principles; it isn't a critique of anyone's work, and it doesn't assume any particular fix.
01

What this is

I put the current LifeForce experience through a UX audit — a structured review of how the live product actually behaves, measured against well-established usability principles rather than personal taste. The goal is a clear, shared picture: where the experience is already strong, and where there's room to make it better. Think of it as a map we can act on, not a verdict.

How it was measured

  • Nielsen's usability heuristics — ten widely-used rules of thumb for whether an interface is easy to understand and use.
  • UX laws — established patterns for how people perceive, decide, and remember (e.g. we can only hold a few things in mind at once).
  • Accessibility (WCAG 2.2 AA) — the standards that keep a product usable for everyone, including older members and low-vision users.

How to read the findings

3
Major — a real obstacle most members will hit. Worth fixing.
2
Minor — adds friction or confusion; fix in normal course.
1
Cosmetic — small polish; fix if time allows.
02

What we're working with

Start here: LifeForce is a genuinely strong program. Most of what follows is about helping the experience live up to it.

Strengths — real advantages to build on

  • A real incentive — medical credit per pay period, plus payroll points. Money is the motivator most wellbeing apps lack.
  • Human, high-touch — a registered-nurse visit. Differentiated and sticky.
  • A built-in engagement engine — the Phase model gives an ongoing reason to return, not one-and-done.
  • A clear qualification path — four discrete steps; the shape is simple.
  • Trusted rails — Truist / Peak Health brand and process.

What makes it genuinely hard to design

  • The reward is genuinely complex — points and per-pay-period credit and phase-set amounts and deadlines. That complexity has to live somewhere.
  • Phase can go down — miss labs or an appointment and you drop to Phase 1. A “protect your standing” story worth telling.
  • Long time-to-value — ~20 business days to the first visit. The gap between effort and payoff needs bridging.
  • External dependencies — Peak owns scheduling and data; several states aren't represented yet.

What I found, on the live screens

The findings aren't abstract — here they are, pinned to the actual current screens. Red = major, amber = minor.

Incomplete headline. “Enroll today, earn up to annually.” — the dollar amount is missing, so the H1 makes a promise it doesn't finish.

Accessibility — a partial read (from screenshots)

Some things need the real build to measure. What's visible now: the muted gray sidebar labels look like they may fall below the minimum contrast, and the heading structure is unclear (several competing bold lines). Contrast, keyboard focus, tap-target sizes and text-zoom all need checking in the live build — worth a proper pass.

Visual design — the look

The current styling is default / framework-standard: flat blue buttons, plain pill “tabs,” bordered boxes, undifferentiated type — so LifeForce reads as a separate module rather than part of the product. This is real and worth improving; it's the “how it looks” side of the picture, alongside the “how it works” findings above.

05

Two kinds of improvement

It helps to separate what's on the table, because they're different kinds of work — and both matter.

1

How it looks — visual design

Color, type, buttons, spacing — bringing LifeForce in line with the rest of the product so it feels native. One of the findings sits here.

10

How it works — the experience

The model behind the screens: how steps are presented, where a member enrolls, whether the reward is clear, whether progress and phase are visible. Most of what surfaced sits here.

Worth knowing as we plan: most of what I found is about how the experience works, not how it looks. Both are worth doing — and the good news is that improving how it works naturally carries the visual refresh along with it.
06

If we start anywhere, I'd start here

Highest-leverage move

Turn enrollment into one guided checklist — a single next action and a visible payoff.

This one change addresses several findings at once — the steps stop behaving like tabs, there's a clear “do this next,” progress becomes visible, and the walls of text give way to a scannable path. It's the single biggest improvement for the least sprawl.

And the bigger idea for the whole program: make the Phase → credit relationship the spine — introduced at enrollment, front-and-center once a member is active. Almost everything else hangs off that one concept.
