EBB · Digital Care Paths · design rationale

Why the Digital Care list and topic home look the way they do

Two pages, one job: move a member toward completing a care path. Not browsing, not reading around it — finishing. This documents the reasoning so the calls are defensible and revisitable.

The shared thesis: completion is where both efficacy and reward live. A member who finishes a path improves clinically and earns the $100; a member who browses, samples, and drifts does neither. So every decision on these two pages spends attention on the next action and the sense of progress, and demotes everything that competes with it — high-level browsing, secondary navigation, ancillary data. Focus is the feature.

1 · The Digital Care list (the library)

What it is: the second-tab library — a member's re-entry point to work already underway, not the app's front door and not a marketplace. High value, but its job is getting people back in, not helping them shop.

Lead with in-progress paths + the reward — resume-first

In-progress paths and the reward summary get the top and the visual weight. The list answers "where do I pick back up?" before anything else.

Why: an unfinished path is an open loop (Zeigarnik effect) and the closer it is to done the harder it pulls (Goal-Gradient). Surfacing it first turns latent intent into a tap. Fewer competing choices at the top means faster re-entry (Hick's Law). And completion is the outcome we're optimizing — clinically and commercially.

Reward shown as progress, not as a headline

The reward is present — "how many paths done · toward $500" — because it's the accelerant that keeps the loop salient. But it reads as progress/accent, never a flashing prize.

Why: the reward is a real motivator, so hiding it wastes it; but making it the loudest thing on the page would invite reward-chasing over health work, which the efficacy guardrail forbids (racing a path ≠ clinical benefit). Present, quiet, in service of finishing.

Browsing stays available, but de-emphasized

You can still browse the full catalog, but it isn't the front door and it isn't weighted equally with resuming. This is a place to get in, not to skim at a high level.

Why: per the IA, high-level discovery lives at the app's front door — the library is for committed work. Inviting casual, rapid browsing here pulls attention out of active paths. Keeping browse present preserves user control (Nielsen: user freedom) without letting it compete.

Jakob's Law / IAUser control & freedom

Rejected: a discovery-first / catalog-marketplace treatment, or browse weighted equally with resume. The risk it creates: path-hopping — members start many paths and finish none. That's the worst outcome for us on both axes: no clinical benefit (efficacy regresses) and no completion (no reward earned, no revenue). The daisy chain only works if the list keeps pointing at the one path to finish next.

2 · The topic home (the program page)

What it is: the inside of a single path. Its only job is to get the member into the program — to the next action — with as little friction as possible.

Module 1 (top): the continue / next-step card — the single primary action

We used to lead with a card offering two buttons — "next step" and "all sessions." We removed that fork and prioritized one action: resume the next session. One confident CTA, top of page.

Why: two co-equal buttons force a decision before the member has done anything (Hick's Law — more options, more hesitation). One large primary action is faster to hit (Fitts's Law) and matches the highest-probability intent — continue where you left off (Goal-Gradient). The page should feel like it already knows what you're here to do.

Hick's LawFitts's LawGoal-Gradient
Module 2 (middle): progress — where you are

A prominent progress read sits second: how far along, at a steady pace. It motivates without being the action.

Why: visible status is both orientation and fuel (Nielsen: visibility of system status; Goal-Gradient again). Framed as pace, not speed — "a steady pace," never a countdown — so it encourages finishing without pushing members to race (which the guardrail shows harms outcomes). At zero it's reframed so it never reads as a failing/empty gauge.

Visibility of statusPace not speed
Module 3 (bottom): the sessions timeline — de-prioritized link, richer visual

"All sessions" used to be a competing button up top; it's now the third module — a timeline showing where you were, where you are, and where you're going. Demoted as navigation, but presented more visually.

Why: the whole-path view is orientation, not the primary path — so it belongs lower. But rendering it as a timeline (done behind, next ahead) does real motivational work (Zeigarnik + Goal-Gradient) and preserves the member's ability to jump anywhere (user freedom). Moving it down removes it from competition with the next-step CTA while keeping it available and legible.

ZeigarnikUser freedomSerial position (bottom = memorable)
Data & resources: removed for now — and if they return, minimized

This version drops the data and resources modules to protect focus. They aren't gone for good: if we bring data back, it's in a much smaller format (e.g., a compact status strip, possibly at the top) so it can't take over the page; resources become a simple, quiet link rather than a module.

Why: a topic page crowded with data and reading material dilutes the one job — getting in (Nielsen: aesthetic & minimalist design; lower cognitive load). But data is not clutter — it's evidence. Under measurement-based care, seeing your own improvement is the strongest non-monetary motivator and the proof EBB is working. So the design tension is placement and size, not presence. Progressive disclosure resolves it: the next action is loud; data and the full session list are available, quieter, on demand.

Rejected: a content-hub topic page (data + resources + articles as co-equal modules); two competing CTAs at the top; the session list as the primary navigation. Each spends attention the member needs for the next action.

3 · Cross-cutting rationalizations

Reasons that apply to both pages — and to the loop they sit inside.

One continuous "get back in" spine

List resumes a path → topic home resumes the session → session → completion celebration → the next path. Every surface points at the next action. The daisy chain isn't a single screen; it's this whole spine, and it only holds if no screen breaks the line by inviting a detour. Consistency across surfaces also lowers learning cost (Jakob's Law).

Progressive disclosure is the organizing rule

On both pages: primary = the next action, loud and singular; secondary = everything else (browse, all-sessions, data, resources), present but visually quieter and often on-demand. This is what lets us reduce clutter without removing capability — the answer to "do we need all that information?" is "not all at once."

Reward discipline is a guardrail, not a style choice

Reward appears as accent and progress on every surface, never as the headline that crowds out clinical content. This is the efficacy guardrail made concrete: engagement mechanics are subordinate to outcomes, and the reward must not tempt members into racing. Success is measured by outcomes holding/improving vs. baseline — not by clicks or speed.

Minimize friction between peaks

The emotional peak of the loop is completion (Peak-End). The topic home's job, between peaks, is to be nearly frictionless — get in, make progress, get out — so the member reaches the next peak. Anything that adds a decision or a distraction taxes that.

Ethical motivation, not dark patterns

We lean on progress and open-loop pull (Zeigarnik / Goal-Gradient) to encourage finishing — but framed honestly around the health work and a real completion reward, never manufactured urgency, loss-aversion pressure, or countdowns. The guardrail and pace-not-speed framing keep the motivation aligned with the member's interest.

Where control lives (IA)

High-level discovery and taxonomy live at the front door / browse — deliberately not in the library or on the topic page. Concentrating discovery elsewhere is exactly what frees these two pages to commit, without apology, to completion.

Grounded in: Nielsen's heuristics (minimalist design, visibility of status, user control), UX laws (Hick, Fitts, Zeigarnik, Goal-Gradient, Von Restorff, Serial-position, Peak-End, Jakob), EBB engagement-behavior principles (habit loop, pace-not-speed), and the EBB efficacy guardrail.
