The short version

Subject matter and colour discipline are in good shape. The library is consistent, inclusive, lifestyle-led, and free of the clinical imagery the standards rule out — only 15 files break subject-matter rules at all.

Lighting is the most-broken rule. 124 files blow out more than 4% of the frame. The standards forbid “cinematic or overly stylized” lighting by name, and heavy backlight and lens flare are the library’s single most common visual habit.

The crop failures are a wording problem, not a photo problem. The standards ask for a “centred focal point.” Photographers centre the body, which puts the face in the top third — and our widest containers crop from the vertical centre. The fix is one sentence, and it is testable before we buy anything.

Finding 1

One in five photographs loses its subject in a full-width hero

Images in the app are never displayed at their own shape. Each container has a fixed shape, and the browser fills it by scaling the photo up and cutting off whatever overflows — from the middle outward. The wider the container, the more of the top and bottom of the photograph disappears.

The square crop fails differently — and on the opposite photographs

Wide shapes cut the top and bottom. The square avatar crop cuts the sides, and it takes out a different set: 7 photographs fail outright and 27 are marginal. The failures are the ones where the subject sits to one side — crop to a square and you get an empty beach, an empty office, a boardwalk with no runner on it. The marginals are mostly groups losing a member: four people become three, a family of five becomes four.

The two sets barely overlap. 50 photographs fail only the wide crop, 6 fail only the square one, and exactly one fails both. So there is no single approved shortlist — a photograph is safe for a shape, not in general.

And the two pull against each other. Of the 41 photographs wide enough to carry a full-width hero, 37% are compromised as a square avatar — against 9% of ordinary card photographs. That isn't a coincidence: hero-capable means a small off-centre subject with clean space beside it, which is exactly what a square crop throws away. Buying more hero-capable photography makes the avatar problem worse. They are two briefs, not one.

Why it happens — and the one sentence that fixes it

The standards say: “centred focal point for subject matter for cropping purposes.” The library obeys that horizontally — which is why the square thumbnail crop produces zero failures. But a photographer centres the body, and that puts the face in the top third of the frame. Every wide container crops from the vertical centre, so the face is the first thing to go.

Replacing “centred focal point” with a measurable band would have caught all 56, and can be checked on a contact sheet in seconds:

cut by a 4:1 hero face must sit here · 31–69% of frame height cut by a 4:1 hero

Frame 131, with the band drawn on. The face sits above it, which is exactly why this photograph is one of the 56 — a wide crop keeps the towel and loses the person.

Proposed rule: the face, or the primary focal point, sits between 31% and 69% of frame height (37.5–62.5% for the 240-tall page header) and within the middle two-thirds horizontally, which is what a square avatar keeps. Photographs composed deliberately for a full-bleed hero — subject at one third, space for type — are exempt from the horizontal bound and get tagged hero-only: never used as avatars.

The cheapest fix costs nothing

Use the split hero — image beside the text rather than text on top of the image — as the default on member-facing pages. It crops nothing at all, so 204 files become eligible and this entire category of failure disappears. Keep full-width heroes for the 41 wide, environmental photographs that can carry them.

Finding 2

Lighting is the most-broken rule in the standards

The standards ask for natural, soft, diffused light and even exposure, and rule out harsh, dramatic or “cinematic” treatment. Heavy backlight and lens flare are the library’s most common habit — measured across every file, not sampled.

This has two costs beyond the brand rule. White text over a high-key photograph is unreadable without a dark scrim laid over the image. And in a grid of cards, images this similar in tone read as a single texture — the eye stops distinguishing one card from the next, which undoes the hierarchy the layout is building.

Finding 3

Colour is mostly disciplined, with two clear exceptions

The standards ask for neutral, warm, restrained colour that harmonises with the interface, and rule out heavy filters and strong casts. 41 files carry a strong warm cast and 33 are oversaturated. Seven sit on studio backdrops in colours we don’t use anywhere in the product.

Two rules that block the product

Worth a decision, not a workaround

Both of these are cases where the standards, read literally, rule out imagery the product needs. Each surface is currently improvising, which is how inconsistency starts.

“Don’t show illness or treatment” vs. the clinical programmes

Taken literally, this removes the entire vocabulary the condition and care-programme entry points need — the honest why you’d start moment. The library reflects the rule perfectly: exactly one symptom photograph and five care photographs, all of which technically break it.

Proposed: a narrow carve-out — non-clinical depictions of how a symptom feels are permitted on condition and programme entry screens; clinical settings stay out of bounds everywhere.

“No forced positivity” vs. rewards and completion

This invalidates around 28 files, and they are nearly the whole celebration set that reward screens, completion moments and earned-badge messages draw from.

Proposed: keep the rule and change the vocabulary. Milestones become quiet earned satisfaction — a look, a pause, a hand resting on a railing — rather than arms thrown wide on a summit. More credible, and closer to what the rest of the standards ask for.

What we’re asking for

Five decisions and a buying brief

  1. Rewrite the focal-point rule.Replace “centred focal point” with the measurable band: the face sits between 31% and 69% of frame height. One sentence, checkable on a contact sheet, catches all 56 crop failures.
  2. Add an exposure ceiling.No more than ~2% of the frame pure white; average brightness under 170. This is the most-violated rule in the set and the easiest to specify.
  3. Two additions to the styling rules.No visible app interface inside a photograph — it dates. And no fine stripes or dense patterns: they shimmer and break up when scaled down to a small thumbnail.
  4. Settle the two conflicts above.Condition and reward surfaces need an answer before the next purchase, or they will keep choosing imagery that argues with their own message.
  5. Prioritise the next batch by gap, not by subject.Tracking and progress (12 files), the honest struggle moment (5), clinical and screening (7, with none of biometrics), strength (3), and wide environmental frames that can carry a full-width hero (41).

Buying brief, in one paragraph

Mid-key, diffused light — no flare, nothing blown out. Face inside the middle third of the frame vertically. Wide enough to leave clean, quiet space on one side for type. Environmental over portrait, real rooms over studio backdrops, and no visible screens. If it’s the fifth photograph of its kind, it replaces one rather than joining them.
